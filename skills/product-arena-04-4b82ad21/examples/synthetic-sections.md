# Introduction

CO2 methanation can store renewable hydrogen in the existing gas grid, but Ni-based catalysts active at low temperature often produce CO as a by-product [@chen2019]. Defect engineering has been proposed to tune CH4 selectivity, yet comparisons are rarely made at a fixed space velocity [@patel2021]. This limits the rational design of selective catalysts. Here we compare NiCo-200 and NiCo-50 at 200–300 °C and 1 bar under the same space velocity, and test whether defect enrichment improves CH4 selectivity.

Our contributions are:

- A controlled comparison of NiCo-200 and NiCo-50 at a fixed space velocity, showing 85% versus 65% CH4 selectivity at 250 °C.
- Evidence that NiCo-200 selectivity decreases above 275 °C, which bounds the operating window.
- An explicit statement of the remaining mechanistic uncertainty and the missing operando measurement.

# Related Work

Two lines of work bracket the present study.

## Defect engineering for Ni catalysts

Chen and Li reported that defect sites raise the low-temperature activity of Ni catalysts [@chen2019]. Patel and Singh showed that bimetallic NiCo catalysts change the product distribution at low temperature [@patel2021]. Both routes, however, compare materials without fixing the space velocity, so the defect effect is mixed with contact-time effects.

## Selectivity metrics for CO2 hydrogenation

Garcia and Rossi reviewed selectivity metrics and showed that raw and conversion-normalised selectivities can rank catalysts differently [@garcia2022]. Patel and Singh reported conversion without a selectivity metric, which prevents direct comparison with selectivity-focused studies [@patel2021]. The metric inconsistency means that cross-paper selectivity claims cannot be compared directly.

The gap is therefore a same-condition comparison of defect-rich and defect-poor NiCo catalysts with an explicit selectivity metric, which we provide here.
